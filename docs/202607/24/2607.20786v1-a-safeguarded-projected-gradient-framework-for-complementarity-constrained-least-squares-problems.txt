# A Safeguarded Projected-Gradient Framework for Complementarity-Constrained Least-Squares Problems 

Lianghai Xiao ∗ Wei Zhang † Jiayi Zhong ‡

Abstract 

Generalized absolute value equations (GAVEs) and linear complementarity problems (LCPs) arise in many equilibrium and optimization models and can both be formulated as least-squares problems over the complementarity set. Building on this shared formulation, we propose a safeguarded projected-gradient framework for both classes with problem-dependent algebraic refinements. We analyze its convergence properties and, under stronger conditions, establish finite recovery of the unique solution. Numerical experiments on GAVE and LCP benchmarks illustrate the practical behavior of the framework, including its high final accuracy in the tested settings and its problem-dependent computational tradeoffs.

Complementarity systems arise in equilibrium models, constrained optimization, and piecewise-linear equations [8,10,12]. 1.1 Related work 

GAVEs and LCPs are closely related classes of piecewise-linear systems. The connection between absolute value equations and complementarity problems is developed in [19], while the theory and classical algorithms for LCPs are treated systematically in [8]; see also the recent AVE survey [12]. Despite their close relationship, the numerical literatures on GAVEs and LCPs often emphasize different algorithmic tools. For AVEs and GAVEs, a major line of research applies generalized, semismooth, or smoothing Newton methods to the piecewise-linear equation [6, 7, 18]. Picard iterations and matrix-splitting schemes instead use fixed-point or relaxed linear solves [22, 25]. Optimization reformulations have also led to successive linearization, complementarity smoothing, and gradient-type methods [1, 17, 24]. These approaches are effective under suitable regularity, contractivity, or unique-solvability conditions. Many of them are formulated for square systems, whereas alternating-projection and randomized-sketching methods explicitly accommodate rectangular GAVEs [2, 23]. LCP algorithms include pivoting methods, projected stationary iterations, and equation-based methods; comprehensive accounts are given in [8, 10]. Projected Gauss–Seidel and projected SOR exploit matrix structure and are classical choices for structured LCPs [8, 9]. Another influen-tial approach uses the minimum or Fischer–Burmeister function to replace complementarity by a nonsmooth equation and then applies semismooth or smoothing Newton steps [11, 14, 21]. Their convergence guarantees depend on the structure of the LCP matrix, the regularity of the equation reformulation, and the globalization strategy. Projection methods provide a closer link between the two literatures. Alcantara et al. reformu-lated a general GAVE as a nonconvex feasibility problem and applied alternating projections [2]. Alcantara and Lee subsequently developed a union-convex analysis for several projection methods, proving convergence to the solution set for P-matrix LCPs and introducing pattern-based accel-eration schemes [3]. These results highlight the usefulness of the union-convex structure. Their methods are built from projections associated with particular feasibility reformulations, and their analysis is formulated in terms of the corresponding sets and fixed-point mappings. Motivated by these developments, we consider a framework that treats GAVEs and LCPs di-rectly through their common complementarity structure while separating the basic iteration from problem-specific refinement. We formulate both problems as linear least-squares problems over the complementarity set and solve them within a common projected-gradient framework. Problem-specific algebraic solves are incorporated as safeguarded refinements, preserving the common con-vergence analysis while exploiting the structure of each problem class. 

1.2 Contributions 

The main contributions are as follows. 

• We develop a safeguarded projected-gradient framework for GAVEs and LCPs that combines a common first-order iteration with problem-specific algebraic refinements while retaining a unified convergence analysis. 2• We establish verifiable conditions for stationary exactness, under which every projected sta-tionary point has zero residual and is therefore a global solution. The results cover both GAVEs and P-matrix LCPs, including biactive points. 

• Building on stationary exactness, we derive global convergence guarantees for the generated sequence. These include convergence to the zero-residual solution set and, in the unique-solution settings, convergence of the entire sequence. We further establish eventual pattern compatibility and finite solution recovery through the full-step refinements. Numerical experiments on GAVEs and LCPs illustrate the practical behavior of the framework, including high final accuracy in the tested settings and performance that depends on the problem structure and comparison method. 

1.3 Organization 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the complementarity pro-jection, stationarity measure, and active-face notation. Section 3 presents the safeguarded projected-gradient framework. The global analysis and the problem-specific exactness and recovery results are given in Section 4. ## 4 Convergence Analysis 

We first establish properties shared by every refinement satisfying (10). The argument proceeds in three steps. The projection inequality yields sufficient descent; telescoping this decrease gives van-ishing projected-gradient residuals and stationarity of every accumulation point; the same estimate gives a worst-case complexity bound. We then specialize the stationarity conditions to GAVEs and LCPs. The active-face normal equation yields facewise least-squares optimality, while explicit rank or range conditions give zero-residual solution-set convergence. Problem-specific generalized nor-mal equations then establish stationary exactness at biactive points under a uniform full-row-rank condition on A − BD or the P-matrix property. Under the stronger square-GAVE condition or the P-matrix assumption, uniqueness upgrades solution-set convergence to convergence of the entire sequence, and the projected candidates eventually have compatible sign or active-set patterns. The next assumption is the stepsize condition used in the analysis.

We first derive normal equations and least-squares optimality on the active face, and then obtain zero-residual convergence under strict complementarity and pointwise rank or range conditions. We then exploit the algebraic structures of GAVEs and LCPs to cover biactive points. Under the subsequent problem-wide conditions, we also show that the projected candidates eventually have compatible sign or active-set patterns. This viewpoint is related to component identification for union-convex problems [3] and active-set stabilization in MPCCs [20].

## 5 Numerical Experiments 

We evaluate the proposed method, denoted by PGDN, on GAVEs and LCPs. Since the two model classes use different variables and residuals, comparisons are made within each class. 

5.1 Implementation details 

The theoretical framework uses the sufficient condition αk < 1/L , whereas the implementation enforces descent directly through a projected Armijo line search and may accept larger stepsizes. The projected-gradient step uses alternating Barzilai–Borwein trial stepsizes [4], globalized by a projected Armijo line search.The trial value is truncated to [ αmin , α max ] and then reduced by the factor β until the projected candidate satisfies the Armijo condition Φ(¯ yk+1 ) ≤ Φ( yk) − σαk

∥¯yk+1 − yk∥2.

Thus, the implementation does not compute the Lipschitz constant L and does not explicitly enforce the theoretical upper bound 1 /L . The default values are α0 = 1, αmin = 10 −12 , αmax = 10 12 ,

β = 0 .5, and σ = 10 −4, unless stated otherwise. The refinement is triggered when either the normalized equation residual is at most newtonTol or the relevant sign/active pattern has been unchanged for signStableIts consecutive iterations. The defaults are newtonTol = 10 −3, signStableIts = 5, and at most maxNewton = 3 inner refinements. Damped candidates are tested in the order γ ∈ { 1, 0.5, 0.25 , 0.1} and retained only if the normalized residual is at most newtonEta = 0 .8 times the best current residual. The final refined point is used only when it improves the projected-gradient candidate, which realizes the safeguard (10). For a GAVE iterate x, the refinement uses the sign matrix D(x) from (3) and solves  A −

Each damped point has the form x + γ(ex − x) and is tested using the true GAVE residual. After acceptance, the complementarity variables are recovered from its positive and negative parts. For the direct LCP solver, let Iw = {i : wi > 0} and Iz = {1, . . . , n } \ I w. Thus a biac-tive index is assigned to the z-branch. The refinement fixes zIw = 0 and wIz = 0 and solves

The results are shown in Figures 7a–7c. The results depend strongly on the benchmark structure. On LCP1, PGDN needs only 7–9 outer iterations and attains LCP residuals between 6 .3 × 10 −15 and 2 .1 × 10 −13 . FB-Newton converges in five iterations, while PGS, PSOR, AMAP+, and APS+ also satisfy the prescribed LCP residual tolerance. In contrast, PG-BT stops at the affine-residual tolerance but not at the stricter LCP criterion, and EGA reaches the iteration limit. The triangular LCP2 strongly favors coordinate methods: PGS obtains zero reported residual in two sweeps, and PSOR takes only 10–11 sweeps. PGDN and AMAP+ also solve every tested in-stance, with zero reported residual. The performance of the two projection metrics differs markedly on this problem: APS+ solves the cases n = 64 and 128, but reaches the iteration limit for n = 256 and 512, whereas AMAP+ remains successful. FB-Newton solves all four instances but its iteration count grows from 92 to 773. PG-BT fails to reach the requested accuracy within 4000 iterations, and EGA does so for the two larger dimensions. On the random LCP3 instances, PGDN, AMAP+, and APS+ have a 100% success rate and median LCP residuals near machine precision at every dimension. AMAP+ and APS+ are substan-tially faster than PGDN here: at n = 512, their median CPU times are 0 .024 and 0 .041 seconds, compared with 3 .16 seconds for PGDN. PGS, PSOR, FB-Newton, and EGA also solve all instances, although at larger median residuals of approximately 10 −9–10 −10 . PG-BT reaches the tolerance on only 20% and 10% of the instances for n = 64 and 128, respectively, and on none of the larger instances. Thus, the active-set refinement in PGDN is effective in recovering highly accurate so-lutions, but its linear algebra is not competitive with the specialized projection and coordinate methods on these random LCPs. 

## 6 Concluding Remarks 

We proposed a safeguarded projected-gradient framework for GAVEs and LCPs that combines a common first-order iteration with problem-dependent algebraic refinements. We established con-ditions ensuring stationary exactness and global convergence, together with stronger convergence and finite-recovery results for selected problem classes. The numerical experiments demonstrate high final accuracy across the tested problems, although computational performance depends on the problem structure and the specialized comparison method. Future work includes inconsistent GAVEs and extensions to nonlinear complementarity-constrained problems.
