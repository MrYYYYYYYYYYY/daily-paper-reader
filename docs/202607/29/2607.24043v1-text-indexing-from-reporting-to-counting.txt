Abstract 

We prove an elementary yet powerful combinatorial lemma: in any rooted tree with 𝐿 leaves, the number of nodes whose depth is smaller than the number of their leaf descendants is at most 𝐿 . For any string 𝑇 of length 𝑛 , a direct application of this lemma to the suffix trie of 𝑇 yields that the number of substrings of 𝑇 whose length is smaller than their number of occurrences in 𝑇 is at most 𝑛 . This combinatorial insight leads to space-efficient data structures with optimal query times for string counting 

problems via the following algorithmic framework: store the counts for the at most 𝑛 “frequent” substrings of 𝑇 in a preprocessing step, and use a reporting query to count for the “infrequent” substrings. Our framework acts as a convenient black box, lifting indexes with reporting time O(| 𝑃 | + | Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 )|) to support counting queries in time O(| 𝑃 |) , where 𝑃 is the queried pattern and Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 ) is the set of occurrences of 𝑃 in 𝑇 . As applications, we show efficient indexes for consecutive occurrences, weighted sequences, strings with utilities, and non-overlapping occurrences. arXiv:2607.24043v1 [cs.DS] 27 Jul 2026 1 Introduction 

Text indexing is a classical problem in computer science with many applications, especially in bioinformat-ics [Gus97] and information retrieval [BR11]. The goal of text indexing is to preprocess a string 𝑇 = 𝑇 [1 . . 𝑛 ]

of length 𝑛 , called the text , into a data structure, called the index , supporting queries for a string 𝑃 = 𝑃 [1 . . 𝑚 ]

of length 𝑚 ≤ 𝑛 , called the pattern . The most typical query types in text indexes are reporting and counting :the former asks for the set Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 ) of all starting positions (occurrences) of 𝑃 in 𝑇 , while the latter asks for 

|Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 )| .The study of text indexing dates back to the early 1970s, when Weiner [Wei73] introduced suffix trees. This foundational data structure occupies O( 𝑛 ) space, can be constructed in O( 𝑛 ) time [Far97], and answers reporting queries in O( 𝑚 + | Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 )|) time and counting queries in O( 𝑚 ) time. Subsequent work on text indexing focused mainly on reducing the space complexity—the main bottleneck in practice—often at the cost of increased query time. Key milestones in this direction include the suffix array [GBS92, MM93], the compressed suffix array [GV05], the FM-index [FM05], as well as the 𝑟 -index [GNP18] and other indexes for highly repetitive texts [Nav21]. The suffix tree of 𝑇 is the compacted trie of all the suffixes of 𝑇 . Starting from the (uncompacted) suffix trie of 𝑇 , the suffix tree is obtained by suppressing all internal nodes with exactly one child: these nodes become implicit , corresponding to points on edges of the suffix tree rather than explicit nodes. The remaining nodes—the root, all branching internal nodes, and all leaves—are called explicit . This compactification leads to O( 𝑛 ) explicit nodes, for a text 𝑇 of length 𝑛 , and therefore to an O( 𝑛 )-space index. Despite the considerable attention given to suffix trees, the following intuition appears to have gone unnoticed. The structural property that allows suffix tries to be compacted from up to Θ(𝑛 2) to Θ(𝑛 ) nodes— namely, that many nodes of the trie share the exact same set of leaf descendants along unary paths—also bounds the number of nodes whose depth is smaller than their number of leaf descendants. Our central contribution is an elementary yet powerful combinatorial lemma formalizing this intuition: in any rooted tree on 𝐿 leaves, the number of nodes whose depth is smaller than the number of their leaf descendants is at most 𝐿 . Applied to the suffix trie of a given string, this yields the following interpretation. 

Theorem 1.1. In any string 𝑇 of length 𝑛 , the number of non-empty substrings of 𝑇 whose length is smaller than their number of occurrences in 𝑇 is at most 𝑛 .

We prove that this bound is asymptotically tight for 𝑇 = (𝑎 1𝑎 2 . . . 𝑎 𝑟 )𝑟 over alphabet Σ = {𝑎 1, 𝑎 2, . . . , 𝑎 𝑟 }.The algorithmic implications of Theorem 1.1 are far-reaching. Theorem 1.1 leads to space-efficient data structures with optimal query times for string counting problems via the following framework: store the counts for the at most 𝑛 “frequent” substrings of 𝑇 in a preprocessing step, and use a reporting query to count for the “infrequent” substrings. Our framework acts as a convenient black box, lifting indexes with reporting time O( 𝑚 + | Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 )|) to counting indexes with query time O( 𝑚 ). More formally, we prove the following theorem. 

Theorem 1.2 (Algorithmic Framework) . Let 𝐷 be a data structure constructed over a text 𝑇 of length 𝑛 , so that, when queried for a pattern 𝑃 of length 𝑚 , it returns an answer of total size 𝑤 in O( 𝑚 + | Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 )| + 𝑤 )

time. Then, there is a data structure ˆ𝐷 of size O(| 𝐷 | + 𝑤 · 𝑛 ) that answers the same queries for 𝑃 in time 

O( 𝑚 + 𝑤 ).

Our framework can be easily extended for efficiently addressing the problem where each occurrence of a pattern has some associated value, and we wish to aggregate these values for all sought occurrences under an associative operator. See Corollary 3.2 for a formal statement. As a first application, we consider the consecutive occurrences indexing problem [BG14, BGP +22, BGPS23, BGVV14, GGSS26, IR09, NT16, Rad23]. In its most basic variant, we are asked to preprocess 𝑇 

and an interval [𝛼, 𝛽 ] into an index, such that, for any pattern 𝑃 , we can report the consecutive occurrences of 1𝑃 in 𝑇 with a distance in [𝛼, 𝛽 ]. While a more general O( 𝑛 log 𝑛 )-space index (that also gets the interval [𝛼, 𝛽 ]

at query time) with optimal reporting queries exists due to Navarro and Thankachan [NT16], the counting version with fixed bounded-gap constraints has no known O( 𝑛 · polylog (𝑛 )) -space solution with O( 𝑚 )-time queries. A black-box application of Theorem 1.2 to the main result of [NT16] yields an O( 𝑛 log 𝑛 )-space index with optimal O( 𝑚 )-time counting queries. We improve the space complexity to O( 𝑛 ). We also present other implications of Theorem 1.2 in data structures for indexing problems, including problems involving weighted sequences [BKL +20, CILP20, GLL +24] and strings with utilities [BCC +24, BCC +25]. For a class of indexing problems in the framework of Corollary 3.2, including indexing strings with utilities, we further show that we can construct an O( 𝑛 )-space index in eO( 𝑛 √𝑛 ) time by using Theorem 1.1 and exploiting the periodic structure of 𝑇 . Our data structure improves over the state-of-the-art index for strings with utilities [BCC +25] polynomially in both the preprocessing time (from O( 𝑛 2) to eO( 𝑛 √𝑛 )) as well as the space–query time trade-off (from O( 𝑛 2/𝜏 ) space and O( 𝑚 + 𝜏 ) query time to O( 𝑛 ) space and O( 𝑚 )

query time). As a bonus, we unify the treatment of several indexing problems, recovering known results for their counting variants by applying our algorithmic framework to their reporting variants; we do so for indexing strings with properties [ACI +08, BKL +20], for indexing for overlapping or non-overlapping occurrences, and for covers [BL ¨ OP02, CP09, Rad23]. Notably, our combinatorial lemma on trees can be generalized to yield the following two corollaries on strings, which have further consequences. 

Corollary 1.3. In any string 𝑇 of length 𝑛 , for any integer 𝜏 ∈ [ 1, 𝑛 ], the number of non-empty substrings 𝑈 

of 𝑇 for which |𝑈 | · 𝜏 < |Occ 𝑇 (𝑈 )| is at most 𝑛 /𝜏 .

Corollary 1.4. In any string 𝑇 of length 𝑛 , for any integer 𝜏 ∈ [ 1, 𝑛 ], the number of non-empty substrings 𝑈 

of 𝑇 for which |𝑈 | < 𝜏 · | Occ 𝑇 (𝑈 )| is at most 𝑛𝜏 .

Corollary 1.3 enables us to transform state-of-the-art reporting data structures (e.g., [GST20, GMT25]) that occupy less than O( 𝑛 ) space (say, they use 𝑜 (𝑛 log 𝑛 ) bits) to support counting queries. Roughly speaking, given an 𝑠 -space data structure, we set 𝜏 = 𝑛 /𝑠 , precompute and store the answers for at most 

𝑛 /𝜏 = 𝑠 substrings 𝑈 of 𝑇 (the ones for which |𝑈 | · 𝜏 < |Occ 𝑇 (𝑈 )| ), and spend O( 𝑚 · 𝑛 /𝑠 ) time at query time for any given length-𝑚 pattern. We use this idea to construct a compact data structure for counting non-overlapping occurrences in near-optimal time, which was mentioned in the literature (cf. [GMT25]) as a “challenging” open problem. Furthermore, Corollary 1.4 enables us to extend the application of our framework to the internal pattern matching (IPM) model [CKM +21, KRRW24], where the pattern is specified as a fragment of 𝑇 . We use this idea to construct an IPM data structure for indexing strings with utilities.

In Section 4, we present the headline applications of our framework and demonstrate the versatility of our approach by recovering several known results under our framework. In Section 5, we prove Corollary 1.3 and showcase an application. In Section 6, we prove Corollary 1.4 and showcase the IPM index for strings with utilities as an application. # 2 The Combinatorial Lemma 

Lemma 2.1. For any rooted tree T with 𝐿 leaves over a set V of nodes, we have 

|{ 𝑣 ∈ V | depth (𝑣 ) < |L( 𝑣 )|}| ≤ 𝐿, 

2where depth (𝑣 ) is the depth of node 𝑣 and L( 𝑣 ) is the set of leaf descendants of 𝑣 .Proof. Let L(T ) be the set of leaves of T . Further, let S = {𝑣 ∈ V | depth (𝑣 ) < |L( 𝑣 )|} . We show that 

|S| ≤ 𝐿 by constructing an injective mapping 𝜙 : S → L(T ) .For any node 𝑣 ∈ S , the condition depth (𝑣 ) < |L( 𝑣 )| means that the number of leaf descendants of 𝑣 is strictly greater than the depth of 𝑣 . We say that a node 𝑣 has in-order rank 𝑖 if it is the 𝑖 th node visited in an in-order traversal of T . Let the leaf descendants of 𝑣 be ℓ𝑣, 1, ℓ 𝑣, 2, . . . , ℓ 𝑣, | L ( 𝑣 ) | , in increasing order with respect to their in-order ranks. The function 𝜙 : S → L(T ) maps each node 𝑣 ∈ S to its 

(depth (𝑣 ) + 1)th leaf descendant according to the above order; i.e., 𝜙 (𝑣 ) = ℓ𝑣, depth (𝑣 )+ 1. Since 𝑣 ∈ S , we have |L( 𝑣 )| ≥ depth (𝑣 ) + 1, ensuring that ℓ𝑣, depth (𝑣 )+ 1 exists within the subtree rooted at 𝑣 . We must show that for any 𝑢, 𝑣 ∈ S with 𝑢 ≠ 𝑣 , we have 𝜙 (𝑢 ) ≠ 𝜙 (𝑣 ). We consider the following two cases: 

Case 1: Neither 𝒖 nor 𝒗 is an ancestor of the other. In this case, the subtrees rooted at 𝑢 and 𝑣 are disjoint. Consequently, their sets of leaf descendants L( 𝑢 ) and L( 𝑣 ) are disjoint. Since 𝜙 (𝑢 ) ∈ L( 𝑢 ) and 

𝜙 (𝑣 ) ∈ L( 𝑣 ), it immediately follows that 𝜙 (𝑢 ) ≠ 𝜙 (𝑣 ).

Case 2: 𝒖 is an ancestor of 𝒗 or vice versa. We assume, without loss of generality, that 𝑢 is an ancestor of 𝑣 —the other case is symmetric. Let 𝑑 𝑢 := depth (𝑢 ) and 𝑑 𝑣 := depth (𝑣 ). Since 𝑢 is a strict ancestor of 𝑣 ,we have 𝑑 𝑢 < 𝑑 𝑣 . The leaf 𝜙 (𝑢 ) is the (𝑑 𝑢 + 1)th leaf descendant of 𝑢 . The leaf 𝜙 (𝑣 ) is the (𝑑 𝑣 + 1)th leaf descendant of 𝑣 . Because 𝑣 is in the subtree of 𝑢 , the leaf descendants of 𝑣 form a contiguous segment within the leaf descendants of 𝑢 (ordered by in-order ranks). Let 𝑘 ≥ 0 be the number of leaf descendants of 𝑢 with smaller in-order rank than 𝑣 . The index of 𝜙 (𝑣 ) among the leaf descendants of 𝑢 is 𝑘 + 𝑑 𝑣 + 1. Since 𝑘 ≥ 0and 𝑑 𝑢 < 𝑑 𝑣 , we must have 𝑑 𝑢 + 1 < 𝑑 𝑣 + 1 ≤ 𝑘 + 𝑑 𝑣 + 1 ⇒ 𝜙 (𝑢 ) ≠ 𝜙 (𝑣 ).Since the mapping 𝜙 : S → L(T ) is injective, the cardinality of the set S must be less than or equal to the cardinality of the set L(T ) , and hence |S| ≤ |L(T )| = 𝐿 . □

The following consequence of the above lemma for strings is immediate. 

Theorem 1.1. In any string 𝑇 of length 𝑛 , the number of non-empty substrings of 𝑇 whose length is smaller than their number of occurrences in 𝑇 is at most 𝑛 .Proof. Apply Lemma 2.1 to the trie of {𝑇 [1 . . 𝑛 ]$, 𝑇 [2 . . 𝑛 ]$, . . . , 𝑇 [𝑛 ]$}, for a unique letter $ that does not occur in 𝑇 . Since, by construction, there is a bijective mapping between the internal nodes of the trie and the substrings of 𝑇 , the claim follows. □

# 4 Applications 

We first present three applications of our framework, where it either yields a novel counting structure for a widely-studied reporting problem, improves upon the best known data structure for a problem, or enriches an existing index with natural non-trivial query types. Then, we recover several known results, thus unifying the treatment of a number of problems. 

4.1 Counting Consecutive Occurrences 

Given a string 𝑇 , an integer interval [𝛼, 𝛽 ], and a pattern 𝑃 , a pair (𝑖, 𝑗 ) is called an (𝛼, 𝛽 )-consecutive occurrence of 𝑃 in 𝑇 if 𝑖, 𝑗 ∈ Occ 𝑇 (𝑃 ), there is no other occurrence of 𝑃 between 𝑖 and 𝑗 , and 𝛼 ≤ 𝑗 − 𝑖 ≤ 𝛽 .We denote this set of pairs by Occ 𝛼𝛽 𝑇 (𝑃 ). Many variants of consecutive occurrences have been studied in the literature [BG14, BGL +24, BGP +22, BGPS23, BGVV14, BLPS99, GGSS26, IR09, KKL07, Mut02, NT16, Rad23]. Here, we consider the counting version of the most basic variant. 

Consecutive Occurrences Counting (COC) 

Preprocess: A text 𝑇 of length 𝑛 and an integer interval [𝛼, 𝛽 ].

However, we can do better. We rely on the following auxiliary data structure.

4.4 More Applications 

Beyond the three headline applications described above, the combinatorial insight (Theorem 1.1) leads to efficient data structures for a wide range of summary statistics queries. Many of them have a known efficient data structure, so our main contribution here is that Theorem 1.2 and Corollary 3.2 provide a simple and 

unified framework for this set of problems. For instance, for Stat (𝑇 [𝑖 . . 𝑗 ]) := 1 and Ç := Í, we get the classical counting of occurrences of 𝑃
